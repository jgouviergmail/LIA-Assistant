# Workboard

## What is the workboard for?
For everything that takes more than one moment. A task list has no lifecycle and nobody carrying it; a reminder rings once and leaves nothing behind; a routine repeats forever without ever finishing. A **ticket** has a title, a description, a priority, a start date and a due date, sub-tickets, comments and a history — and it moves across seven columns, from a plain idea to done. Open it from **Workboard** in the header.

## What do the columns mean?
**Idea**: noted, not committed to — LIA never runs one. **To do**: ready to be picked up. **In progress**: being worked on, by you or by LIA. **Waiting**: stopped, waiting on a decision from you. **To confirm**: LIA has built something it cannot do alone and is asking — this column only appears while it holds a ticket. **Validating**: LIA has delivered, it is up to you to accept. **Done**: finished, including something you decided not to do — the reason stays in the ticket's history.

## How do I hand a ticket to LIA?
Open the ticket and set **Held by** to LIA — or pick LIA when you create it. From then on LIA takes it in its own time: it picks up one ticket at a time, runs it, and comments what it found or produced. The ticket moves to **Validating** with the answer, or comes back to you with a question. You can also press **Run now** to put it at the front of the queue. What LIA reads is your own words — the title, the description, and for a sub-ticket the parent's title: comments never enter the instruction, so nobody else can steer a run through them.

## What happens when LIA needs my agreement?
It stops and asks on the ticket instead of doing it or giving up. The ticket lands in **To confirm**, and LIA writes exactly the card you would have seen in the chat: what it is about to do, field by field. Reply with one comment — *yes*, *no*, or what you want changed — and that is the whole answer: the ticket goes back to LIA on its own. If you changed something, LIA rebuilds and asks again rather than assuming; what you approved is what runs, and nothing wider.

## Can I share a ticket with someone?
Yes, with anyone you are connected to on this instance. Set **Held by** to them and the ticket appears on their board too — it is the same ticket, so the two of you can never read two different versions of it. They can move it, comment, set their own notifications and hand it back; the title, the description, deletion and reassignment stay yours. If the connection ends, every ticket held either way comes back to its owner automatically. What you cannot do is hand a ticket to someone else's LIA: the instruction is yours, and running it unattended with their tools would put their data in a comment you read.

## Will LIA remind me?
It brings back what deserves a word, with the reason it did: a due date that has passed, one coming up, a question of its own left waiting too long, or a result nobody has validated. It quotes what is actually waiting rather than paraphrasing it, and it does not repeat itself — a ticket it mentioned stays quiet for a couple of days. Each ticket carries its own **Notify me in the chat** switch, so a ticket you would rather handle silently stays on the board without ever speaking. One message is never filtered: being handed a ticket, because nobody subscribes to a ticket they do not know they hold yet.

## What does it cost, and where do I see it?
A run LIA does for you is billed to your account like any other, and the ticket adds it up: the panel shows the last run's tokens and cost, and the ticket's total across its life. A run refused by your usage ceiling is not a failure — it is postponed, the attempt is given back, and the ticket says so. **Settings › Workboard** shows the whole board at a glance: totals, what is overdue, what LIA holds, what is on your plate, an exact count per column, and your capacity against its ceiling.

## Are there limits?
Yes, and they are all published rather than hidden: how many tickets an account may own, how many sub-tickets a ticket may have, how many times LIA may run the same ticket in its life, how long a run may take, and how long a hidden run transcript is kept after a ticket closes. Titles, descriptions and comments have a length. Your instance's administrator sets them; a refusal always names the limit it hit instead of failing silently.
